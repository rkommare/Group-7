# 1. Problem and Users
Plants are a common household item that can be decorative or functional. Although they mostly require just two factors to survive, sunlight and water, the breadth and variety of plant species available makes it deceptively difficult to maintain them. Currently, there are many resources both online and in traditional media sources on the subject of plant care, however, there is a distinct lack of such information for helping people decide what to purchase initially. One of the most commonly overlooked factors in plant maintenance is the cardinal direction that the plant is facing, in turn impacting the amount and intensity of sunlight it receives on a daily basis. South facing windows get bright direct sunlight, east and west facing windows receive moderate indirect sunlight, and north facing windows have much less sunlight. This is important because some plant species prefer a lot of light while others are actually harmed by too much light. For example, a cactus would do well in a south facing window but be starved of sunlight by a north facing window. The goal of our product is to guide beginner to intermediate plant growers on purchasing plants that are ideal for their particular space, thus reducing headaches and disappointments in the future for ill-fitting plants.  

# 2. Product
The product we created is termed Fern-Ware, which has an intuitive end-to-end web architecture created to bridge the gap between human intuition and complex botanical care requirements. By taking a user’s organic, conversational description of their living space and unique lifestyle routines, Fern-Ware can transform natural language input into deeply explainable, scientifically grounded houseplant recommendations. Rather than having users to navigate overwhelming catalog taxonomies, our platform acts as an empathetic digital botanist, decoding subjective environment descriptions into actionable, personalized indoor greenery solutions. At the core of Fern-Ware’s analytical engine lies a processing framework that meticulously evaluates nuances of the user's microclimate and care capabilities. For every environmental factor, ranging from ambient light and humidity to irrigation frequency, the backend parser returns a comprehensive data suite. This includes a proficiency level that is categorized, an overarching statistical model score, a precise score margin, and a full probability label distribution. To achieve this, Fern-Ware employs a reactive, highly responsive JavaScript frontend layer. The client-side application intercepts, validates, and preserves all high-confidence inputs provided by the user, ensuring seamless persistence across the interaction lifecycle. When critical environmental variables are missing, ambiguous, or incomplete, the JavaScript interface dynamically triggers follow-up questions to harvest the necessary data points without frustrating the user.

# 3. NLP Method and Evaluation
Because the core of the product relies on natural language text processing, we opted to use a simplified user interface structure that employs large text boxes and simplified options. When a user types in an input, it is saved as a string and then passed to a custom function that handles the raw processing and begins correlations. Information related to the environmental conditions is compared to a .json database of watering, temperature, and humidity. This is incorporated as a looped system where the BART model attributes confidence, and if the value is less than 0.65, prompts the user for clarification. This is to avoid needless guesswork that could negatively impact the user’s experience, recommending plants that may not necessarily be good fits for their space. When the value does exceed 0.65, attributes are added to the user’s profile to save for later, so that it does not forget and need to ask for clarification of topics already discussed. Finally, upon completion of all 3 requirements being fulfilled by the user, the program identifies a list of plant species that satisfy them all and are displayed on screen.  

# 4. User Evidence
In order to test the effectiveness of such a product, we performed tests with the chatbot on two groups of people: Those that were familiar with plants, and those that were not familiar with plants. While we were more interested in the second group, the first group gave us feedback on the overall correctness and usefulness of the product itself. The most important difference between the two groups we observed was the specificity in the language when talking with the chatbot. Group 1 tended to use specific, detailed language that made it easier for the program to identify ideal species and reduce back and forth. Group 2 tended to use more vague, subjective language, requiring multiple iterations with the chatbot for it to retrieve all necessary information from the user.  For example, phrases such as “bright” and “lots” with no qualifiers. This required the chatbot to prompt the user for more information, which could feel tedious after many iterations. What exacerbated the problem was users would respond with equally short responses, creating a cycle of repeated questioning. Taken together, these user tests heavily guided the decision making of the tone and direction of the chatbot. Namely, an emphasis on friendliness with a push to obtain more than one information piece if possible. For the model itself, that resulted in a fine-tuned BART-MNLI to interpret varied language with the same underlying meaning and adding a mention detection so an unmentioned condition is not treated as a prediction. Also, the inclusion of a model-score and score-margin checks to identify uncertain classifications, and a pendingCondition state so responses such as “lots” are interpreted in context.  

# 5. Ethics and Limitations
Several ethical considerations were made with our product, all centered around the main risk of presenting an uncertain prediction as a reliable recommendation. This could lead to both wasted time and money from users who purchased plants expecting them to thrive in an apparent unideal environment. Responsible use of data also played a role. For data rights, record the source and license of plant descriptions and training data; avoid using copyrighted material without permission. For privacy, the app does not require names or account details, processing descriptions locally and avoids retaining user messages or unnecessary logs. For bias, synthetic training data may overrepresent certain phrases and fail on unfamiliar wording, dialects, or living conditions. When the model is wrong, model scores are not calibrated probabilities, and the classifier may guess when information is missing.Missing or low-confidence conditions trigger a clarification question rather than an immediate recommendation. Users can correct detected conditions, and partial matches are clearly identified.Recommendations are informational—not guarantees of plant survival, pet safety, or medical safety. Finally, incorporation of a fail-safe principle: If a required condition is missing or uncertain, ask the user instead of guessing.
